The medical case for AI, starting with radiology

Radiology is where AI in medicine has the strongest evidence. In South Africa it matters most where there are too few specialists and too many scans.

I'm a marketer, not a clinician, so this is not medical advice. I'm interested in this subject because the best AI stories are the ones with proof behind them, and radiology has more proof than almost any other part of medicine.

Why radiology comes first

A medical image is data. X-rays, mammograms and scans arrive in huge volumes, they follow patterns, and reading them well takes years of training. Many countries do not have enough radiologists to read everything quickly. Software that can sort, flag and double-check images fits that problem closely.

The strongest evidence so far: breast screening

The Mammography Screening with Artificial Intelligence trial, known as MASAI, ran inside Sweden's national screening programme. It randomly assigned more than 105,000 women either to AI-supported screening or to the standard approach of two radiologists reading each mammogram. The results, published in The Lancet Digital Health in 2025, were striking. The AI-supported group's cancer detection rate was 6.4 per 1,000 women compared with 5.0 per 1,000, an increase of about 29%. The radiologists' screen-reading workload fell by about 44%. The reports on the trial say false positives did not increase.

The AI did not replace the doctors. It helped decide which screenings needed closer attention, and radiologists stayed in the loop. A few cautions apply. This was one country and one AI system, which its maker also publicised. Longer-term outcomes still need to be studied. It is strong evidence, and it is not the final word.

The South African use case: tuberculosis

By some measures tuberculosis is South Africa's leading cause of death, and many people who have it show no symptoms, so they never get tested. One answer has been to take X-ray machines to communities in mobile units and screen people on the spot. The bottleneck is reading the images, because specialists are scarce. Computer-aided detection software now supports several of these programmes, many of them funded by international aid groups.

The evidence is encouraging. A 2021 study found that a group of AI programmes detected TB on chest X-rays as accurately as experienced radiologists, and some did better. A 2022 study in the journal Radiology, from researchers at Google Health, found that a deep-learning system matched radiologists' performance, including in a test group of gold miners in South Africa, who are at high risk. A South African company, Nexus AI, says its tool screens a chest X-ray in about 45 seconds and operates in more than six African countries. Its founders are clear that it is meant to support radiologists, not replace them. They point out that sub-Saharan Africa has fewer than one radiologist for every 300,000 to 500,000 people. The Department of Health has also said it plans to use the same kind of software for silicosis, a lung disease common among miners.

The benefits in plain terms

  • Earlier detection. In the Swedish trial, more cancers were found at screening.
  • Faster reading. An image can be assessed in seconds, which means urgent cases can be put at the front of the queue.
  • A second pair of eyes. Software does not tire at the end of a long shift, and it is consistent.
  • Reach. Screening can happen in a clinic or a van without a specialist on site.
  • Less pressure on scarce experts. Their time goes to the cases that need judgement.

The limits

  • AI is not a doctor. A flagged image still needs a clinician and often a confirmatory test.
  • Local validation. A system must be tested on the patients, equipment and disease rates of the place where it will be used.
  • Errors both ways. Missed cases and false alarms both carry a cost, and both have to be monitored.
  • Rules and staffing. South Africa's regulations make mobile X-ray use harder than it needs to be, and a shortage of radiographers, who take the images, is a separate barrier.
  • Privacy and accountability. Patient data must be protected under South African law, and someone must be clearly responsible when a tool is wrong.

What strikes me, coming from marketing, is trust. The AI that will win in medicine is not the loudest one. It is the one that proves itself in a trial, works alongside professionals and is honest about where it fails. That is a good standard for every industry, mine included.
