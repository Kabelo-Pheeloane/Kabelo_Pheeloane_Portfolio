How AI will change agriculture and energy in South Africa

South Africa's two oldest pressures, food and power, are meeting the same new tool. Here is where the evidence is real and where the hype runs ahead of it.

Farms and the power grid have one problem in common: they are unpredictable. Rain, pests, sun, wind and demand all move, and the people running them have to decide today what will happen next week. Artificial intelligence is, at its core, a prediction tool. That makes it a natural fit, and in both sectors South Africa already has real examples.

Agriculture: from walking the rows to seeing every tree

The clearest local example is Aerobotics, a Cape Town company founded in 2014 by James Paterson and Benji Meltzer. It combines imagery from drones, satellites and smartphones with machine learning to count trees, flag missing or sick ones, track pests and disease and forecast yields. It serves growers in roughly 18 countries and raised US$17 million in a Series B round led by Naspers Foundry in 2021. An earlier industry report said about 40% of South African macadamia producers and a fifth of citrus growers were using it at the time.

Why does that matter? Scouting an orchard by foot is slow, and a problem can spread before anyone sees it. The software does not replace the farmer or the agronomist. It tells them which tree to walk to. It also moves the analysis earlier, so that size, colour and quality can be estimated before harvest and not discovered at the pack-house.

My view is that the next wave is more of the same: yield and quality forecasting from a phone, irrigation scheduled against weather and soil data, earlier warnings for pests and disease, and better-timed decisions about when and where to sell. These are extensions of tools that already exist, not science fiction.

Energy: the grid is becoming a forecasting problem

Eskom's Medium-Term System Adequacy Outlook for 2026 to 2030 describes a fleet of about 65 GW, still dominated by coal but with growing solar, wind and battery storage. Its most interesting finding is not about the total. It shows days with a surplus of solar in the middle of the day and a shortage in the evening, a pattern analysts call a "duck curve", unless a lot more battery storage is added. In the words of one analyst, the question moves from how many megawatts we have to how flexible and well-timed they are.

That is exactly where AI is strong. Local researchers are already building the tools. A 2026 study in AIMS Energy used deep-learning ensembles to forecast hourly electricity demand from South African plant data. Another 2026 study built physics-informed neural networks to forecast national demand using Eskom's own data from 2021 to 2026, and compared them with Eskom's official projections. Energy experts also point to AI deciding when to store surplus solar and when to release it into the grid.

Those are research results, not proof that the grid will run itself. As one energy expert put it, AI has real potential here but it is not a magical solution.

Where the two meet

This part is my own reasoning, not a reported fact. Farms are heavy energy users: irrigation pumps, cold rooms and pack-houses all draw power. If a grower can forecast both the weather and the grid, they can run pumps when solar is abundant, chill fruit when power is cheap and avoid peaks. Food and energy planning start to look like one system, and prediction is the thread that links them.

What could go wrong

  • Connectivity. Many farms and rural areas still have patchy coverage, and cloud tools need a signal.
  • Who gets left out. The best-documented tools serve commercial fruit and nut growers. Smallholders need products built for them and priced for them.
  • Data ownership. Orchard data is valuable. Farmers should know who owns it and how it is used.
  • Models built elsewhere. A model trained on another country's conditions needs testing on ours.
  • The basics still matter. AI cannot fix ageing power stations or a weak balance sheet. It helps you run what you have more wisely.

What to watch

  • Whether forecasting tools move from research papers into daily grid operations.
  • Whether battery storage grows fast enough to flatten the evening gap.
  • Whether agritech built for large growers finds affordable versions for smaller farms.

South Africa has a habit of solving hard problems because it has to. Food and power are as hard as they come, and the tools to attack both are already here.
